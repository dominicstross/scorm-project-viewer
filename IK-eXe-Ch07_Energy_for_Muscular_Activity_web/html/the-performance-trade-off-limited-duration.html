The Performance Trade-off – Limited Duration

The Performance Trade-off – Limited Duration


In the study of kinesiology, we must respect the Inverse Relationship: as power output increases, the duration of that output must decrease. The Phosphagen system is the ultimate architectural example of this trade-off. While it provides the most immense power of any engine, it does so with an extremely limited fuel tank.

The muscle’s local supply of Creatine Phosphate is small and is depleted rapidly during all-out effort. Physiological data confirms that at Very High Intensity, the system only sustains movement for 7-12 seconds. Even at moderate intensity, the local supply is exhausted within 15-30 seconds. Once this 12-second "tipping point" of maximal effort is reached, the CP stores are depleted, and the body must transition to slower, secondary energy systems. Analyzing why a sprinter’s 100m power output might drop at the 11-second mark reveals this depletion in real-time.

Study Questions

If a sprinter’s 100m time is 11.5 seconds, analyze why their power output might drop significantly at the 11-second mark. 

Based on CP depletion levels, the Phosphagen system only lasts 7-12 seconds at max intensity; at the 11-second mark, the CP "fuel tank" is nearly empty, causing power to fall.

Define the "Inverse Relationship" in kinesiology. 

It is the principle that as power output goes up, the duration that the power can be sustained must go down.

Contrast the duration of the system at "Very High Intensity" versus "Moderate Intensity." 

At Very High Intensity, it lasts 7-12 seconds; at Moderate Intensity, it lasts 15-30 seconds.

Why is the Phosphagen system's "fuel tank" described as limited? 

Because the local supply of Creatine Phosphate in the muscle is very small and is consumed rapidly.

Evaluate the "tipping point" of 12 seconds in maximal effort. 

It is the point where CP is largely exhausted, forcing the body to switch to slower energy systems to continue moving.

Describe what happens to the CP supply during an "all-out effort." 

It is depleted rapidly to meet the massive energy demand.

What must the body do once the Phosphagen system is exhausted? 

Transition to other, slower energy systems like the Glycolytic or Oxidative systems.

Why can't the Phosphagen system sustain a 1-minute sprint? 

The local CP stores are insufficient; they are completely depleted long before the 1-minute mark (usually by 12 seconds).

Is the CP supply in the muscle large or small? 

Very small.

Analyze the relationship between power and the "fuel tank" size. 

High power demands a high rate of fuel consumption; because the local CP tank is small, the duration of that high power is very short.
