17.5 Step 5 Training Load and Repetitions

Introduction

Effective resistance training hinges on the precise manipulation of training load and repetitions. Load, the weight assigned to an exercise, is a fundamental variable that dictates the physiological demands placed on the body. Understanding how load interacts with repetitions is crucial for achieving specific training goals, whether it's building muscular strength, hypertrophy, power, or endurance. This section delves into the methodologies for quantifying training load, the intricate relationship between load and repetitions, and the practical strategies for assigning and progressing loads based on various training objectives.

Accurate assessment of an athlete's capabilities, such as their 1-repetition maximum (1RM), is essential for tailoring training programs. Various testing options, including direct 1RM testing and estimations from multiple-RM tests, provide valuable data for load assignment. However, it's vital to recognize the limitations of standardized tables and equations, as individual responses to training can vary significantly. By understanding these principles, strength and conditioning professionals can optimize training programs to maximize athlete performance and minimize the risk of overtraining.

Training Load and Repetitions

The amount of weight used in resistance training, known as load, is a key factor. It determines the stress placed on the body. To measure this stress, we use terms like "volume-load," which is the total weight lifted, and "repetition-volume," which is the total number of repetitions. There's an inverse relationship between load and repetitions: heavier weights mean fewer reps. The 1RM, or the maximum weight lifted for one rep, is often used to set training loads. Tables that link %1RM to the number of reps are useful, but they have limitations because they don't account for individual differences. Testing can determine the athlete's 1RM directly or estimate it through multiple-RM tests. The 1RM test is best for experienced athletes. When testing, core exercises are preferred. 

Loads are assigned based on training goals, using the RM continuum or %1RM. For power training, lighter loads are used to maximize speed. To prevent overtraining, loads should be varied, with heavy, light, and medium training days. The 2-for-2 rule helps determine when to increase loads.

Summary

Training load, defined as the weight used in resistance exercises, is a critical component of effective strength and conditioning programs. It is directly related to the repetitions performed, with an inverse relationship existing between the two. The concepts of volume-load and repetition-volume help quantify the amount of work done during training. Determining an athlete's 1RM, either through direct testing or estimation, is crucial for assigning appropriate training loads. While %1RM-repetition tables provide guidelines, they have limitations due to individual variability and should be used as a starting point.

Training goals dictate the specific load and repetition schemes used. Strength and power training typically involve heavier loads and fewer repetitions, while hypertrophy and muscular endurance training utilize moderate to lighter loads with higher repetitions. Power training emphasizes movement velocity, requiring lighter loads to maximize speed. To prevent overtraining, varying training loads across different days is essential. The 2-for-2 rule provides a practical method for progressing training loads as athletes adapt.
